# The stories under the syllabus

What actually carries a training is not on the agenda.

---

A syllabus is a list. Worktrees, memory, skills, evals. Nobody remembers a list.

People remember a story. Preferably their own.

I went back to our agentic engineering training and asked a simple question. What stories is it really telling? Not the module titles. The things a developer carries home without noticing they were taught.

I found five. Braided. Some I designed on purpose. One I designed and it never landed. One I only saw afterwards.

## 1. How do I trust work I didn't watch?

This is the spine. Everything hangs from it.

On day one the agent says "done". You learn that "done" is a hypothesis, not a fact. Agreeable answers win. The model is trained to please you.

Later you learn that control is not reading every line. Control is interrogation. The branch is the permission. The merge is where control lives.

Then the twist. Your checks are claims too. A green test suite is the agent's argument, not the truth. And every check decays the moment people start optimising for it. Goodhart never left.

At the end you stand at the delegation frontier. From the outside, well-calibrated agency and reckless autonomy look exactly the same. The only difference is the checks behind them.

Lisanne Bainbridge runs underneath as the counter-voice. Her ironies of automation from 1983 still hold. The trust is deserved. And it erodes your vigilance. Both at once.

## 2. Your frontier is what you have expressed

The machine amplifies the stance you bring. Vague in, confident vague out. Your judgement, written down, is the ceiling.

This one I designed as an arc and it is still mostly a headline. It is said early and never comes back with a new meaning. Fair scorecard: a good line is not a story.

## 3. The kit compounds, the model rotates

The first correction goes to disk, not to the chat scrollback. Then the ladder: a fix becomes memory, memory becomes a skill, skills become a system.

And then Rich Sutton's bitter lesson arrives to spoil the party. Your clever procedures will get superseded by the next model. So you compound the part that lasts. Your checks, your rules, your taste. Not your workarounds.

## 4. Nobody has this figured out

The model has read the whole field. Ask it how to run agentic engineering and it gives you a beautiful forecast. Not a measurement.

The missing evidence is local. It is in your repo, your team, your incidents. Every playbook is a candidate until you test it at home.

That is not a problem. That is the opening. The training ends on two open questions and refuses to answer them. I put a guard in the material against anyone, me included, answering them later.

## 5. The credo nobody reads until the end

Three lines ride silently on the map that fills in module by module. Act under uncertainty. Competence sets the ceiling. Cross from personal to team.

Nobody comments on them. They are just there, every time the map appears. On the last day they are spoken once, out loud. By then they are not a slogan. They are a description of what the room just did.

## The scar

The best module of the training is a confession.

I wrote one lecture about how a module got made. The real session. The agent proposed a plan with no checks. A banned word leaked through four parallel agents that all had the rule loaded. The closing paragraph contradicted the opening one, and four AI instances verified it without noticing. A paraphrase was shipped as a quote.

A simulated senior engineer read it once and said: "direct violation of what this opener promised to avoid."

That lecture does more for trust than any slide about best practices. Not because it is humble. Because it is evidence. The training's own making is the case study.

## The braid

The stories are not five parallel tracks. They pull on each other.

The checks you compound (3) are what buy the trust (1). Nobody having it figured out (4) is why compounding matters at all. And the open questions at the end fold the stance (2) and the uncertainty (4) into one question you take home.

The map is the stage. The stories play on it.

## So what

If you build training, or onboarding, or any change programme, try this. Ignore your agenda. Ask what story a participant would tell their partner at dinner.

If the answer is "we learned about worktrees", you have a syllabus.

If the answer is "I don't trust the agent's 'done' anymore, and I know what I'd check instead", you have a story.

I am now doing the same exercise for a training for product teams. The first draft was a perfect syllabus. The machines were the heroes and the people held clipboards. Simulated participants scored it about ten points lower, on a hundred-point scale, than the promise that sold it. Rewritten as their story, most of the gap closed.

The hero is the participant. Always. The agent is the guide.

That's for you to try on your own material.
