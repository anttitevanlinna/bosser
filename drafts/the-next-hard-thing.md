## Part 1 — Antti's text (verbatim sentences, reordered)

A few musings about what is going to be the next hard thing about working with LLMs.

LLMs can create anything and everything, especially digital stuff.

They can conjure up any idea, a solution to any problem.

Which means that everybody will have a lot of solutions.

Now that is of course the same as the saturation of the market.

So once problem solving becomes easy enough, even for business problems, then we are going to be back at the fundamentals of what makes for a great business.

The point is that the LLM will give you averages.

It will give you many plausible things, but the LLM still, without grounding to customers, cannot determine what is optimal and what is not optimal.

It can make a guess, but still it's a guess.

And we come back to differentiation, choosing your battles and being yourself, curating, choosing where we are going to be the absolute best in our chosen field.

So back to the notion of Playing to Win, differentiation by Porter, and these kinds of things.

So that's one axis: the axis of curating for something distinct.

I named the distinctiveness, or uniqueness, as the first vector or axis.

The second one is precision.

Doing precisely right for the chosen customer, in the chosen situation, on the chosen job to be done.

The precision of rightness is again something that the LLM cannot figure out on its own.

However much you hypothetically ask for solutions and ask for the LLM to do the right thing.

The third is creativity.

And this is from me, in my humble opinion.

Again, a third similar axis that defeats the "anything is plausible."

What happens with LLMs is that it gives the most fitting answer.

You really have to force the LLM to become creative.

It's totally possible to get the LLM to exhibit creativity and get out of the average behavior, but that is something where you really have to twist the LLM's arm to get there.

And that's the point, actually, of the whole blog post.

It is a classic good old: LLM gives you the average, LLM gives you the common.

But you have to do the work to actually steer it to become distinctive, differentiated, precise, and creative all at the same time.

## Part 2 — Fable's commentary (DELETE BEFORE PUBLISHING)

**What you have.** The arc is already sound: abundance → saturation → back to fundamentals → three axes → the thesis. The strongest line is the last one — "you have to do the work to actually steer it" — and right now the post earns it. The "averages" paragraph works well placed before the axes, because it becomes the root cause all three axes hang from.

**Things possibly missing, in rough priority order:**

1. **The "what do you do about it" is absent.** Your own structure standard is setup → patterns → what to do. The post ends on "you have to do the work" but never says what the work looks like. Even 3-4 lines would land it: How do you steer toward distinctiveness? (You bring the strategy — the LLM executes inside choices it didn't make.) Toward precision? (You bring customer grounding — real conversations, real feedback, real jobs-to-be-done.) Toward creativity? (You push past the first answer — reject the most fitting response, ask for the weird ones, recombine.) Without this, a reader nods and changes nothing.

2. **No concrete example.** Every axis is asserted, none is shown. One before/after would carry the whole post: the average answer an LLM gives to a real prompt vs. what it gave after you steered it. You've surely got one from your own work. This is also the difference between "analyst voice" and your "I tried this" voice.

3. **There's an asymmetry between the axes worth making explicit — or deliberately ignoring.** Distinctiveness and precision are *informationally* impossible for the LLM: it doesn't have your customers, your context, your choices. No future model fixes that, because the missing ingredient is not capability, it's grounding. Creativity is different — it's *behaviorally* default-average but reachable with effort, as you say yourself ("totally possible... twist the arm"). If you name this, you preempt the obvious objection "won't better models solve this?" Answer: for creativity, partially. For the other two, never — the data isn't in the model.

4. **The saturation point could be sharpened with the classic strategy observation:** when everyone has the same tool, the tool stops being the advantage. LLM output becomes table stakes; what's scarce shifts to the inputs only you have (your choices, your customers, your taste). That's the Porter link made concrete rather than name-dropped.

5. **Who is the reader?** The post floats between "businesses in general" and "someone prompting an LLM." The thesis line ("steer it to become distinctive, precise, creative all at the same time") suggests the reader is the operator — the person actually working with the LLM. If so, consider making the axes personal: *your* battles, *your* customer, *your* refusal to accept the first answer.

6. **Small structural option:** the three axes are three altitudes of the same departure from average — distinctiveness is strategy-level (which battle), precision is execution-level (exactly right for whom), creativity is idea-level (out of the most-fitting answer). One line noting they're the same move at three altitudes would tie the post together. Or it's over-engineering — your call.

**Title candidates (yours to reject):**
- The Next Hard Thing
- The LLM Gives You the Average
- Steering Out of the Average
- Everyone Has Solutions Now

**Watch-outs for the rewrite:** "vector or axis" — pick one word and stay with it. And the double framing "distinctive, differentiated" in the thesis line says the same thing twice; it might be intentional rhythm, might be transcription residue.
